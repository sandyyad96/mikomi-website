20 Common Mistakes Indians Make While Learning Japanese

The pronunciation, script, grammar and study-habit mistakes we see most often in our own classroom — with concrete wrong-vs-right examples, not vague advice.

These are the mistakes we correct most often in our own classroom — not generic tips copied from elsewhere. Every one of them is fixable, and most of them are fixable faster than you'd expect once you actually see the wrong-vs-right example spelled out.

ℹ️

Why Indian learners hit these specific mistakes

Many of these patterns come directly from habits that work perfectly well in English or Hindi but don't transfer to Japanese — different sound inventory, different script system, different sentence order. Recognising why a mistake happens makes it much easier to catch yourself doing it.

Pronunciation Mistakes

1. Pronouncing つ (tsu) as "choo" or "soo." つ is a single, sharp "tsu" sound with no equivalent in English or most Indian languages, so learners default to the nearest familiar sound. Practice: つくえ (tsukue, desk) should sound crisp, not "chukue" or "sukue."

2. Not distinguishing short and long vowels. おばさん (obasan, "aunt") and おばあさん (obaasan, "grandmother") differ only in vowel length, but that difference changes the entire word. Skipping this distinction is one of the most common sources of embarrassing misunderstandings for beginners.

3. Turning ん (n) into a full "na" or "nu" sound. ん is a nasal stop, not a syllable with its own vowel — さん (san) should end crisply on the nasal, not stretch into "sanu."

4. Applying English-style word stress. English stresses specific syllables heavily (PHO-to-graph); Japanese pitch accent is flatter and works differently, rising and falling in a pattern across the whole word rather than punching one syllable. Saying ありがとう (arigatou) with a heavy English-style stress on "GA" makes it noticeably harder for native speakers to recognise, even though every individual sound is technically correct.

5. Confusing similar-sounding hiragana/katakana pairs. シ (shi) and ツ (tsu), ソ (so) and ン (n) look and sound similar to new learners and get mixed up in both reading and pronunciation. Slow down on these specific pairs rather than assuming speed will fix it — it won't until the distinction is deliberately drilled.

Script & Writing Mistakes

6. Trying to skip hiragana/katakana to get to "real" study faster. This is the single most common mistake we see, and it backfires every time — weak script fluency slows down every single thing that follows, since you end up sounding out basic words letter by letter for months longer than necessary.

7. Writing kanji without learning proper stroke order. Stroke order isn't just tradition — it builds the muscle memory that makes both writing and fast recognition possible later. Kanji written out of order often looks visibly "off" to native readers even when the final shape is roughly right.

8. Relying on romaji long-term. Romaji is a fine bridge for your very first few days, but real Japanese — and the JLPT itself — uses hiragana, katakana and kanji exclusively. Learners who lean on romaji past the first couple of weeks consistently take longer to reach reading fluency.

9. Confusing lookalike katakana under reading pressure. Even learners who've "learned" katakana often freeze on ソ (so), ン (n), シ (shi) and ツ (tsu) when reading at natural speed. This needs dedicated flashcard drilling specifically on these four characters, not just general katakana review.

Almost every mistake on this list traces back to one habit: trying to shortcut the foundation to get to the "interesting" part faster. The foundation is what makes the interesting part possible. — Astha Pokhriyal, Founder & Lead Instructor

Grammar Mistakes

10. Confusing は (wa) and が (ga). Neither Hindi nor English has a direct equivalent to Japanese's topic-marking particle system, so this distinction can't be learned through direct translation — it has to be absorbed through repeated exposure to correct example sentences. 「私は学生です」(I am a student — topic) versus 「だれが来ましたか」(Who came? — subject) use different logic that takes deliberate practice.

11. Using casual/plain form in situations that call for polite form. だ/plain-form endings in a work or formal context land very differently than intended — even when the grammar is technically correct, the tone reads as inappropriate. 「わかった」("got it," casual) versus 「わかりました」("understood," polite) are grammatically similar but socially very different — default to です/ます form until you're certain a casual context calls for otherwise.

12. Direct word-for-word translation from English or Hindi sentence structure. Japanese follows Subject-Object-Verb order, not Subject-Verb-Object like English. "I eat rice" becomes 「私はご飯を食べます」(literally "I rice eat") — translating word-by-word in English order produces sentences that are simply not valid Japanese.

13. Misusing を (wo/o) and に (ni). を marks a direct object, に often marks a destination or point in time — mixing these up is extremely common because English "to" and "at" don't map cleanly onto either. 「学校に行きます」(I go to school) uses に for destination; 「本を読みます」(I read a book) uses を for the object being acted on.

14. Overusing 私 (watashi) and other pronouns. Japanese frequently drops the subject entirely when it's clear from context — repeating 私 in every sentence, a habit carried over from English, sounds unnatural and overly emphatic to native speakers.

15. Confusing transitive/intransitive verb pairs. Verbs like 開ける (akeru, to open something) and 開く (aku, for something to open) look similar but behave grammatically differently. Mixing them up is a very common intermediate-level mistake that even learners well past N5 still trip over.

Vocabulary & Study Habit Mistakes

16. Memorising vocabulary lists without example sentences. A word learned in isolation is far easier to forget than one learned inside a sentence you understand. Always pair new vocabulary with at least one example sentence, ideally one you wrote yourself.

17. Learning too many new words per day with no review system. Cramming 50 new words in one sitting feels productive but most are forgotten within days without spaced repetition. Fewer new words per day, reviewed consistently, beats large batches studied once.

18. Staying passive — avoiding speaking out of fear of mistakes. Listening and reading-only study builds recognition but not production — they're genuinely different skills. Someone who can understand a conversation perfectly can still freeze completely when asked to produce their own sentence on the spot. You have to speak — badly, at first — to build the actual skill you'll need in an interview or workplace.

19. Only practising with slow textbook audio. Textbook audio is deliberately slowed down and clearly enunciated for learners; real workplace and daily-life Japanese moves faster and blends sounds together. A learner who scores well on textbook listening exercises can still struggle to follow a real conversation between two native speakers. Mixing in natural-speed native audio, even just 10 minutes a day, closes this gap far faster than textbook audio alone.

20. Studying in inconsistent, long bursts instead of daily short sessions. A single 3-hour session once a week is far less effective than 30-45 minutes every day — consistency builds retention in a way that occasional intensity simply doesn't. This is the same principle behind our 6-month JLPT N5 roadmap.

How to Fix These, Starting Today

💡

Pick one, not twenty

Don't try to fix all 20 at once — that's its own version of mistake #17. Pick the single mistake from this list that felt most familiar, fix that one deliberately for a week, then move to the next. Sustainable correction beats an overwhelming overhaul every time.

  • If you recognised a pronunciation mistake, drill it specifically with audio repetition — don't just "be more careful," actively practise the exact sound pair that trips you up.
  • If you recognised a script mistake, go back to basics before moving forward — our free Hiragana and Katakana workbooks are built exactly for this kind of reset.
  • If you recognised a grammar mistake, write five of your own example sentences using the correct pattern rather than just re-reading the rule.
  • If you recognised a study habit mistake, change the system, not just your willpower — shorter daily sessions with review, not longer sessions with more discipline required.

Frequently Asked Questions

What is the biggest mistake beginners make learning Japanese?

Trying to skip or rush through hiragana and katakana to get to "real" vocabulary and kanji faster. This almost always backfires — weak script fluency slows down every single thing that follows, since you end up sounding out basic words letter by letter for months longer than necessary.

Why do Indian learners specifically struggle with は and が?

Hindi and most Indian languages don't have a direct equivalent to Japanese's topic-marking particle system, so the は/が distinction has no natural translation to lean on — it has to be learned through pattern exposure rather than direct translation logic.

Is it bad to use romaji at all?

Romaji is fine as a brief bridge in your very first days, but relying on it beyond the first few weeks slows down reading fluency significantly. JLPT tests and real-world Japanese use hiragana, katakana and kanji exclusively, so extended romaji use delays the skill you'll actually need.
