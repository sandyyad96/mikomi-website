Top Japanese Interview Questions (With Sample Answers)

The questions you'll actually be asked, in Japanese, romaji and English — plus how to answer them, the business etiquette that's judged alongside your words, and practical tips from real interview prep sessions.

In a Japanese interview, what you say and how you say it are judged almost equally. A technically correct answer delivered too casually, or without the expected etiquette, can cost you the offer just as easily as a wrong answer. This guide covers both halves: the questions themselves, and the behaviour around them.

What's Different About Japanese Interviews

If you've only interviewed in India before, a few things will feel unfamiliar:

  • Politeness level (keigo) is part of the evaluation. Using overly casual language, even accidentally, can read as disrespectful regardless of your answer's content.
  • Punctuality is strict. Arriving even a few minutes late without prior notice is taken seriously — aim to arrive 10 minutes early.
  • Modesty is valued over self-promotion. Confident but understated answers generally land better than assertive, achievement-heavy answers common in Western-style interviews.
  • Group interviews are common for SSW and factory-style roles, where several candidates are interviewed together — staying attentive and respectful even while others answer matters.
  • A rirekisho (Japanese-style resume) is standard and often reviewed before or during the interview itself.
ℹ️

A note on accuracy

Interview style varies by company, industry and interviewer. The patterns below are commonly observed norms, not universal rules — treat them as a strong default, and adjust based on the specific company's signals.

How to Answer the Toughest Questions

A few of the questions above trip up candidates more than the rest. Here's how to approach them.

自己紹介をお願いします — Please introduce yourself

This is almost always the opening question, and it sets the tone. Keep it to 30-45 seconds: your name, where you're from, your relevant experience or training, and one sentence on why you're interested in this role. Avoid reciting your entire life history — this isn't the place for it.

💡

Sample structure

「はじめまして。〇〇と申します。インドから来ました。〇〇の仕事の経験があります。日本語を一生懸命勉強しています。よろしくお願いします。」
("Nice to meet you. My name is ___. I'm from India. I have experience in ___. I'm studying Japanese hard. Thank you for your consideration.")

あなたの短所は何ですか — What are your weaknesses?

Don't say you have no weaknesses — it reads as dishonest or unreflective. Name a real but manageable weakness, then immediately explain what you're doing about it. This shows self-awareness, which is valued more than the "weakness" itself.

なぜ転職したいのですか — Why do you want to change jobs?

Never criticise your previous employer, manager or company, even if your reasons are legitimate. Frame your answer around what you're moving toward (growth, a new skill, this specific opportunity) rather than what you're running from.

何か質問はありますか — Do you have any questions for us?

Always have at least one question ready. Asking nothing can read as disinterest. Good options: asking about training provided in the first weeks, the team you'd be working with, or what a typical day looks like. Avoid leading with questions about salary or holidays as your very first question.

なぜこの会社を選びましたか — Why did you choose this company?

Generic answers ("Japan has good salaries") are the most common way candidates fumble this question. Interviewers want to hear that you did at least basic research — mention something specific: the industry, the type of work, or what you learned about the company from your sending organisation or recruiter. It doesn't need to be elaborate, just specific and honest.

残業は可能ですか — Are you able to work overtime?

Answer honestly, but frame willingness positively where genuinely true: 「はい、必要であれば残業できます」("Yes, I can work overtime if necessary"). If you have real constraints, it's better to raise them clearly and respectfully than to overcommit and struggle later — employers generally respect honesty here more than an eager but unrealistic "yes to everything."

5年後、自分はどうなっていると思いますか — Where do you see yourself in 5 years?

This is testing whether you're thinking long-term about a career in Japan rather than treating the job as a short stopgap. A strong answer connects your growth to the company or industry — improving your Japanese to a higher level, taking on more responsibility, or moving from SSW Type 1 toward Type 2, for example — rather than vague personal goals unrelated to the role.

Business Etiquette Essentials

Bowing (お辞儀 / ojigi)

BowAngleWhen to Use
会釈 (eshaku)~15°Casual greeting, passing in a hallway
敬礼 (keirei)~30°Standard interview greeting and thanks
最敬礼 (saikeirei)~45°Deep apology or highest formal respect

For most of the interview — entering, introducing yourself, and leaving — a keirei (30°) bow is appropriate. Keep your back straight, pause briefly at the bottom, and don't rush it.

Business card exchange (名刺交換 / meishi koukan)

If you're given a business card, receive it with both hands, take a moment to read it before putting it away respectfully (not in a back pocket), and don't write on it in front of the giver. As a candidate you may not always exchange cards, but knowing the etiquette signals cultural awareness if the moment arises.

Keigo basics

Japanese has distinct politeness registers. For an interview, the safe baseline is teineigo (polite form, です/ます endings) throughout. You don't need advanced keigo to interview well — consistently polite, simple Japanese is far better than attempting advanced honorifics incorrectly.

Punctuality & entering the room

Arrive 10 minutes early. If it's an in-person interview, knock, wait for permission to enter, greet with 「失礼します」("shitsurei shimasu" — excuse me for intruding), and wait to be told where to sit rather than sitting down immediately.

Dress code & appearance

Conservative business attire is the safe default even for factory, hospitality or caregiving roles where the actual job involves a uniform — dark-coloured formal wear, neat and simple. Avoid strong perfume/cologne, flashy accessories, or casual footwear. For online interviews, the same standard applies from the waist up, and check your background and lighting in advance.

Practical Interview Tips

  • Use the PREP structure for longer answers: Point (your answer), Reason (why), Example (a specific instance), Point (restate briefly). This keeps answers organised and easy to follow in a second language, and stops you from rambling when nervous.
  • Practise out loud, not just in your head. Reading an answer silently feels easy; saying it under pressure, with someone watching, is a completely different skill — rehearse with a friend, mirror, or your instructor.
  • Record yourself and listen back — you'll catch filler words, pacing issues and pronunciation habits you don't notice while speaking in the moment.
  • It's fine to ask for repetition. Say 「もう一度お願いします」("mou ichido onegai shimasu" — could you say that again, please) rather than guessing and answering the wrong question, which looks worse than asking for clarification.
  • Keep answers concise. 30-60 seconds per answer is usually enough — rambling reads as nervousness, not thoroughness, and can lose the interviewer's attention.
  • Prepare your rirekisho details in Japanese in advance — dates, previous employer names, job titles — so you're not translating on the spot mid-interview.
  • Sit with good posture throughout, hands resting calmly, and avoid crossing your arms — small physical cues are read as confidence or nervousness just as much as your words.

After the Interview

In many cases, especially through a sending organisation, a short thank-you message the same day is appreciated, though not always expected — check with your recruiter or agency on local norms for the specific company. Keep it brief: thank them for their time, restate your interest in the role, and avoid asking about the outcome directly. Waiting periods vary widely, from a few days to several weeks depending on the company and how many candidates they're evaluating — following up once, politely, after a reasonable gap is acceptable if you haven't heard back.

Mistakes to Avoid

  • Memorised-sounding answers. Interviewers can tell when an answer is recited word-for-word — practise the structure, not a fixed script.
  • Badmouthing a previous employer. Even valid complaints reflect poorly on you in this context.
  • Overusing casual speech even briefly, especially under nervousness — stay in teineigo throughout.
  • Avoiding eye contact excessively. Modesty is valued, but completely avoiding eye contact can read as evasiveness rather than respect.
  • Not preparing a closing question. Silence when asked "any questions?" is a missed opportunity to show genuine interest.

Frequently Asked Questions

What Japanese level do I need for a job interview?

Most SSW interviews are conducted at a level consistent with JLPT N4-N5, using clear, simple Japanese. Engineer/IT interviews often expect N3 or higher, sometimes with technical vocabulary specific to the role. Interviewers generally speak slowly and clearly to candidates at the SSW level.

Is it okay to answer in simple Japanese if I'm not fluent?

Yes. Interviewers assessing SSW and entry-level candidates expect simple, clear Japanese, not native-level fluency. A short, correct, confidently delivered answer is far better than a long answer full of mistakes and hesitation.

What if I don't understand a question?

It's completely acceptable to politely ask the interviewer to repeat or rephrase the question — for example, "Mou ichido onegai shimasu" (Could you say that again, please). Asking for clarification is seen as more professional than guessing and answering the wrong question.

How many times should I bow in a Japanese interview?

There's no fixed count — a respectful bow (keirei, about 30 degrees) when entering the room, when introducing yourself, and when leaving is generally sufficient. What matters more than the exact count is that each bow is unhurried and sincere rather than rushed.
