Why Japan Is Hiring From India Right Now

Japan is one of the most rapidly aging societies in the world, and its workforce has been shrinking for years across sectors like caregiving, construction, food service, manufacturing, agriculture and IT. To address this, the Japanese government introduced the Specified Skilled Worker (SSW / Tokutei Ginou) visa in 2019, specifically to bring in overseas workers for labour-short industries — alongside the older Technical Intern Training Program (TITP) and the long-standing Engineer/Specialist in Humanities visa used mostly for IT and white-collar roles.

For Indian job-seekers specifically, cooperation between India and Japan on skilled and semi-skilled worker mobility — including government-to-government frameworks around technical training — has grown steadily over the past several years, alongside strong private-sector demand in IT, engineering and manufacturing. Together, this has opened a real, well-documented pathway into Japan that did not exist at this scale a decade ago.

It is not, however, a shortcut. Every legitimate route into a Japanese job requires some combination of language ability, a recognised skills qualification, and patience with a multi-step immigration process that typically takes the better part of a year from a standing start. Candidates who treat it as a quick fix — or who fall for agents promising a fast, guaranteed placement — are the ones who end up frustrated, or worse, out of pocket. This guide walks through the process the way we walk our own students through it: step by step, with realistic numbers and the mistakes we see most often.

ℹ️

A note on accuracy

Visa rules, salary levels, and government schemes in Japan are updated periodically. Wherever this guide gives a number or a rule, treat it as a general, commonly-cited range rather than a guarantee — and always confirm current details on Japan's official Immigration Services Agency website or with a licensed advisor before making decisions.

Who Is Eligible to Work in Japan From India?

There is no single "Japan work visa" — eligibility depends entirely on which route you take, and conflating the requirements of one category with another is one of the most common sources of confusion we see in first counselling calls. That said, a few baseline expectations apply across almost every route:

  • Age: There is no universal legal age cap for most categories, but in practice, employers and sending organisations skew strongly toward candidates between 18 and 35, partly due to the physical nature of many SSW roles and partly due to how training programs are structured.
  • Education: Requirements vary enormously by route — some categories require no formal degree at all, while the Engineer visa typically requires a relevant bachelor's degree or equivalent work experience.
  • Japanese language ability: Almost every legitimate route requires you to clear a language checkpoint before you can even sit your skills exam or apply for a visa.
  • Health: A medical fitness certificate is a standard requirement, especially for physically demanding SSW categories like caregiving, construction and agriculture.
  • Clean background: A police clearance certificate is required for almost all routes.
You do not need to already speak Japanese to start this journey. Almost every student who has gone on to work in Japan through our guidance started from zero — the difference was that they started early and stayed consistent. — Astha Pokhriyal, Founder & Lead Instructor, Mikomi Institute of Japanese Language

What Japanese Level Do You Actually Need?

This is the question we get asked most often, and the honest answer is: it depends on your route, but you will need more than "a little Japanese" for almost every legitimate job offer.

  • SSW roles generally require passing either the JFT-Basic (Japan Foundation Test for Basic Japanese) or JLPT N4, roughly equivalent to being able to handle everyday conversations and basic workplace instructions, plus a separate skills exam specific to your industry.
  • TITP placements often accept a lighter starting level, since intensive language and skills training continues after arrival in Japan — but candidates who arrive already comfortable with hiragana, katakana and basic grammar adapt visibly faster.
  • Engineer/Specialist visa roles have no single fixed legal language requirement, but in practice most Japanese employers expect at least N3, and often N2, for day-to-day workplace communication — unless the role is inside one of the relatively rare English-first multinational offices.

In every case, JLPT N5 is the starting line, not the destination. It gives you the alphabet (hiragana and katakana), basic grammar patterns and a core vocabulary — the foundation everything else is built on. The Step-by-Step Process

Here is the realistic sequence most successful candidates follow, from a standing start to landing in Japan.

1

Learn Japanese and get certified

Start with hiragana and katakana, then build toward JLPT N5 and N4 (or JFT-Basic, depending on your target route). This is the single biggest time investment in the whole process — and the step most applicants underestimate.

2

Choose your visa category and target industry

Match your education, physical readiness and long-term goals against the SSW, TITP and Engineer routes covered above.

3

Take the required language and skills exam

SSW and TITP routes require passing an industry-specific skills exam in addition to your language test. These are held periodically, sometimes in India and sometimes only in Japan or other countries — plan around the exam calendar for your target industry.

4

Find an employer or registered sending organisation

This can happen through a licensed recruitment/sending organisation, a direct employer application, or in some cases a government-linked placement program. Verifying that any organisation you work with is properly registered is one of the most important checks in this entire process.

5

Sign your employment contract

Read it carefully — salary, working hours, accommodation arrangements and contract length should all be spelled out clearly before you proceed.

6

Certificate of Eligibility (CoE) application

Your employer or supporting organisation typically files this on your behalf with Japan's Immigration Services Agency. It confirms you meet the requirements for your specific visa category.

7

Visa application at the Embassy/Consulate

Once your CoE is approved, you apply for your actual visa stamp at the Japanese Embassy or a Consulate-General in India.

8

Pre-departure orientation and departure

Many sending organisations run a short pre-departure briefing covering workplace culture, banking, housing and day-to-day life in Japan before you fly out.

How Long Does the Whole Process Take?

This is the part applicants most often get wrong — they budget for the visa paperwork and forget how long the language and exam preparation genuinely takes.

StageTypical Duration
Reaching JLPT N5–N4 (structured coaching)3–8 months, depending on your starting point and study pace
Finding an employer / sending organisation1–3 months
Certificate of Eligibility processing1–3 months
Visa stamping at Embassy/Consulate1–2 weeks
Total, realistic end-to-endRoughly 8–14 months

These are general planning ranges, not guarantees — individual timelines shift based on exam schedules, processing backlogs, and how consistently you study. Candidates who begin structured Japanese classes early and treat exam preparation seriously tend to land on the faster end of this range.

⚠️

Only work with registered organisations

One of the most common and most costly mistakes we see is candidates paying large, vague "placement fees" to unregistered agents. Before paying anyone, verify their registration status, ask for an itemised, written cost breakdown, and be cautious of anyone who guarantees a job offer or visa approval — no legitimate organisation can guarantee immigration outcomes.

Your First Month in Japan: What to Expect

Getting the visa is not the finish line — your first few weeks in Japan involve their own checklist, and knowing it in advance removes a lot of unnecessary stress.

  • Residence card (在留カード): You'll typically receive this at the airport on arrival if your stay is longer than 3 months. Carry it at all times — it functions as your primary ID in Japan.
  • City hall registration: Within your first two weeks, you'll usually need to register your address at your local municipal office. Your employer or sending organisation will normally guide you through this.
  • Bank account and My Number: Opening a Japanese bank account and receiving your "My Number" (a national ID number used for tax and social insurance) are early priorities, since payroll is usually set up around them.
  • Health insurance and pension enrolment: Most work visa categories require enrolment in Japan's national health insurance and pension systems — a portion of your salary will typically go toward these deductions.
  • Workplace orientation: Expect a period of on-the-job training even in SSW roles, alongside close supervision as you adjust to workplace norms, punctuality expectations and communication style.
  • Cultural adjustment: Homesickness and culture shock are normal in the first month. Workers who stayed in loose contact with a support network back home — and who had already built basic conversational confidence before arriving — tend to settle in faster.

This is also where the language groundwork you did in India pays off the most. Workers who arrive able to handle basic daily conversation settle into their new routine noticeably faster than those relying entirely on translation apps and colleagues' patience.

9 Common Mistakes Indian Applicants Make

  1. Starting the visa paperwork before starting Japanese study. Language is almost always the longest step — start it first, not last.
  2. Assuming JLPT N5 alone guarantees a job. N5 is a foundation, not a qualification most employers hire on.
  3. Working with unregistered or unverified agents. This is the single biggest source of financial loss we see among applicants.
  4. Ignoring the Japanese-style resume (rirekisho) format. A generic Western-style CV can work against you with Japanese employers who expect a specific layout.
  5. Underestimating the industry skills exam. Passing JLPT N4 does not mean you're ready for the caregiving or construction skills test — these require separate, dedicated preparation.
  6. Not budgeting realistically for the full cost. Applicants who only plan for the visa fee are consistently surprised by coaching, exam and agency costs.
  7. Choosing a visa category on salary alone. A slightly higher SSW wage can matter less than the long-term path to renewal, upskilling or permanent residency — think in terms of your five-year trajectory, not just your first paycheck.
  8. Skipping business etiquette and keigo preparation. Interview performance in Japan is judged on politeness and workplace manners as much as on language accuracy; a technically correct answer delivered too casually can still cost you the offer.
  9. Not verifying scheme details on official portals. Government-linked programs and their terms can change — always cross-check current details on the relevant official government website before paying any fee, and treat any claim that a scheme is a "limited-time" or "guaranteed" opportunity as a red flag.

Frequently Asked Questions

Can I get a job in Japan without knowing any Japanese?

In a handful of English-first tech roles, yes — but for the vast majority of jobs, including every SSW and TITP category, some functional Japanese is required before you can even sit the qualifying exam. Most successful applicants start from zero and reach the required level through 4 to 8 months of structured study.

Is JLPT N5 enough to get a job in Japan?

JLPT N5 alone is rarely enough on its own. Most SSW categories ask for the JFT-Basic test or JLPT N4, and most Engineer/Specialist visa employers expect N3 or higher for workplace communication. N5 is the right starting point, not the finish line.

How long does the whole process take from zero Japanese?

For most candidates starting from zero, a realistic end-to-end timeline is roughly 8 to 14 months: several months of language study, one to three months to secure an employer, one to three months for Certificate of Eligibility processing, and one to two weeks for visa stamping. Individual cases vary.

What is the difference between the SSW visa and TITP?

SSW (Specified Skilled Worker) is designed for people who already have a practical skill level, with pay generally aligned to regular local wages for the role. TITP (Technical Intern Training Program) is framed as on-the-job training rather than standard employment, with historically more restrictive conditions. Many workers move from TITP into SSW after their training period.

Do I need a university degree to work in Japan?

It depends on the visa route. Most SSW categories do not require a degree — they require passing a skills exam. TITP is similarly open to a wide range of educational backgrounds. The Engineer/Specialist in Humanities visa, used for IT and white-collar roles, generally does require a relevant bachelor's degree or several years of directly relevant work experience.

How much does it cost to get a job in Japan from India?

Costs vary widely depending on your language coaching choice, exam fees, document processing and whether you use a placement or sending organisation. Always get a written, itemised cost breakdown before paying anyone, and verify that any agency you work with is properly registered.

Is the SSW visa a path to permanent residency in Japan?

SSW has two tiers. The first tier generally does not lead directly to permanent residency and has a maximum stay period. The second tier, available in fewer industries, allows longer stays and can open a path toward permanent residency over time. Rules in this area are updated periodically, so always confirm current details on Japan's official Immigration Services Agency website.
